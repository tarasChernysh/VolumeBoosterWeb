A volume booster is generally safe when you use it moderately. The risk to your hearing comes from how loud and how long you listen, not from the app: the WHO says adults can listen at 80 dB for up to 40 hours a week, and far less time at higher levels. Boost in small steps, check iPhone's headphone level meter, and turn it down at the first sign of distortion.

What a volume booster actually does

A volume booster adds digital gain: it makes the audio signal itself louder before it reaches your speaker or headphones. In EQfy - Louder Volume, the Volume Boost dial goes from 100% (no extra gain) to 200%, which adds up to +12 dB on top of your iPhone volume. That's a big jump, which is the point when a recording is too quiet. It's also why it deserves some care.

Two different things can go wrong with too much boost, and they need different answers:

  • Your hearing. This depends on the sound level at your ear and how long you're exposed to it.
  • Sound quality and your speaker. This depends on how much gain the audio and the speaker can handle before they distort.

Is a volume booster safe for your hearing?

Hearing damage from loud sound builds up with regular exposure, and it can be permanent. The World Health Organization estimates that over 1 billion young adults are at risk of permanent, avoidable hearing loss from unsafe listening (WHO fact sheet). The WHO also notes that even a one-time exposure to extremely loud sound can damage the inner ear.

The key idea is a weekly sound allowance. The louder the sound, the less time you can safely listen. These figures come from the WHO safe listening Q&A:

Sound levelSafe listening time per weekWHO example
80 dB40 hoursDoorbell
85 dB12 h 30 minHeavy traffic (inside a car)
90 dB4 hoursShouted conversation
95 dB1 h 15 minMotorcycle
100 dB20 minHair dryer

Notice how fast it drops. Going from 80 dB to 90 dB cuts your safe weekly time from 40 hours to 4.

What's a safe listening level for headphones?

The WHO and the International Telecommunication Union published a standard for personal audio devices, Safe listening devices and systems (H.870). It describes two modes: 80 dB for 40 hours per week for adults and 75 dB for 40 hours per week for children. If you track your listening, that weekly budget is the number to keep in mind.

Practical rules for boosting safely

The WHO's safe listening advice translates easily to a phone with a booster app:

  1. Keep the volume down. The WHO suggests setting your device to no more than 60% of maximum, or staying below an 80 dB average if you use an app to measure it.
  2. Use well-fitted, noise-cancelling headphones. Blocking outside noise means you don't need to turn the music up as far.
  3. Give your ears breaks. The WHO says regular breaks from loud sound help the sensory cells in your ears recover.
  4. Watch for warning signs. Persistent ringing, trouble hearing high-pitched sounds or following conversations are reasons to get your hearing checked.

You'll often hear the "60/60 rule": listen at no more than 60% volume for no more than 60 minutes, then take a break. It's common advice and an easy habit, but it's a rule of thumb, not an official WHO rule. The 60% part matches the WHO's advice. The 60 minutes is a simple way to remember to take breaks.

One catch with boosters: once you add gain, "60% on the volume slider" no longer tells you how loud the music really is. A boosted song at 60% can be as loud as an unboosted one at a much higher setting. So when you boost, judge by the measured level, not by the slider position.

Use iPhone's built-in hearing tools

iPhone already has good tools for this, and they work alongside any app:

  • Live headphone level. In Control Center, tap the add button, choose Add a Control, and add Hearing (under Hearing Accessibility). It shows a Headphone Level meter in decibels while you listen.
  • Listening history. In the Health app, tap Browse › Hearing › Headphone Audio Levels to see your exposure over days and weeks.
  • Headphone notifications. If you've listened loudly for long enough to affect your hearing, iPhone tells you and lowers the volume the next time you connect headphones.
  • Reduce Loud Audio. In Settings › Sounds & Haptics › Headphone Safety, set a maximum level. iPhone reduces headphone sound above it.
  • Speaker Volume Limit. In Settings › Sounds & Haptics › Volume Limit, you can cap the built-in speaker too.

Apple documents all of these in Check your headphone audio levels on iPhone and Adjust the volume on iPhone. One honest limitation: Apple says measurements are most accurate with Apple or Beats headphones, and for other headphones the level is estimated from your iPhone volume. That estimate may not fully reflect a boost added inside an app, so with third-party headphones, be extra conservative. If you have an Apple Watch, the Noise app can also warn you when your surroundings get loud.

Can a volume booster damage your speakers?

Digital audio has a hard ceiling. A song that's already mastered loud sits close to it. Add a lot of gain and the loudest peaks can hit the ceiling and get cut flat. That's clipping, and you hear it as crackling, buzzing or a harsh, gritty sound that wasn't in the original. Heavy bass at full volume can also make a small phone speaker rattle as it reaches its physical limit.

Nobody can promise that any amount of boost is safe for every speaker, so the practical rule is simple. If you hear distortion, turn it down. Back off when you notice:

  • crackling or popping on loud beats,
  • buzzing or rattling in the bass,
  • vocals that sound harsh or "broken",
  • a sound that gets worse as you push the dial.

Clean sound at 150% beats distorted sound at 200%, for your ears and your speaker. If your iPhone speaker sounds distorted even without any boost, follow Apple's guide for no sound or distorted sound from the iPhone speaker.

Boosting is a useful tool for quiet recordings, noisy places and weak speakers. Used gently, with breaks and an eye on the decibel meter, it's a reasonable way to get more volume.
